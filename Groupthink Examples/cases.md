# Groupthink Activity: Screen-by-Screen Scripts

Four cases, one per student. Five screens each, with a button between screens so every reveal
lands on its own click. Target: about 90 seconds per case.

| # | Case | Groupthink symptom |
|---|------|--------------------|
| 1 | Submarine B-59, October 1962 | Direct conformity pressure |
| 2 | The peanut guideline, 2000 | Ignoring disconfirming evidence |
| 3 | Volkswagen's cheating software, 2008 to 2015 | Mindguards |
| 4 | The Mozart effect, 1993 to 2009 | Illusion of invulnerability |

---

# CASE 1: Direct Conformity Pressure
### Submarine B-59, October 1962

**SCREEN 1** *(image: case1-hero)*
> One man stood between the world and complete nuclear annihilation.

`[ Reveal Your Case ]`

**SCREEN 2** *(image: case1-submarine)*
> Inside a USSR submarine in 1962, pressure was building. While the crew was growing delirious
> from days within a cramped, 120°F hull, they suddenly received warning that American carriers
> were dropping depth charges on them. They had only minutes to react!

`[ What Happened Next ]`

**SCREEN 3**
> The captain and political officer both jumped to the same conclusion: they must fire their
> nuclear-armed torpedo at the Americans. Otherwise, they'll be destroyed! But the sub could only
> fire nukes if all three commanding officers turned their keys.

`[ Who Was the Third Officer? ]`

**SCREEN 4** *(image: case1-arkhipov)*
> The captain and the officer applied **direct conformity pressure** to the third officer,
> Vasili Arkhipov, to fire the nuke. All three of them knew it would bring about all-out nuclear
> war. Fighting the pressure of his own commanding officers, Arkhipov stood firm and refused to
> turn his key.

`[ So What Were the Americans Doing? ]`

**SCREEN 5** *(image: case1-surfacing)*
> It turned out the Americans were dropping *practice* charges, small signaling explosives meant
> to make the sub surface, not sink it. Nobody was trying to kill them. Arkhipov single-handedly
> saved the world from nuclear war, and the U.S. government didn't learn how close it came for
> another forty years.

> ⚠️ **One fix to your draft:** the U.S. Navy knew exactly where B-59 was. Forcing it to surface
> was the whole point of the practice charges, so "they had no idea a USSR sub was even nearby"
> isn't accurate. The line above keeps the same gut-punch ("nobody was trying to kill them") and
> adds a real kicker: Washington didn't find out until the 1990s.

# CASE 2: Ignoring Disconfirming Evidence
### The peanut guideline, 2000

**SCREEN 1** *(image: case2-hero)*
> The most cautious advice in modern medicine helped create an epidemic.

`[ Reveal Your Case ]`

**SCREEN 2** *(image: case2-committee)*
> In 2000, America's most trusted pediatricians faced a frightening question: why were a handful
> of children suddenly dying from peanuts? They had almost no research to go on, so the committee
> reached for the safest-sounding answer available. Keep peanuts away from babies until age 3.
> They called it "an abundance of caution."

`[ What Happened Next ]`

**SCREEN 3**
> Everyone fell in line. Parents, daycares, schools, and airlines pulled peanuts out of the first
> three years of American childhood. And over the next decade, peanut allergy in U.S. children
> **more than tripled.**

`[ Did Anyone See It Coming? ]`

**SCREEN 4** *(image: case2-bamba)*
> Yes, and the evidence was sitting in plain sight. Israeli babies were eating a peanut puff
> called Bamba before their first birthday, and had roughly **one-tenth** the peanut allergy rate
> of nearly identical children in Britain. But the committee had already committed. Through
> **ignoring disconfirming evidence**, the guideline stood for eight more years.

`[ How Did It End? ]`

**SCREEN 5**
> In 2015 the decisive trial landed: feeding babies peanuts *early* cut their odds of peanut
> allergy by **81%**. Today's official advice is the exact reverse of the 2000 advice. Start
> peanuts at 4 to 6 months. An entire generation got the opposite of what it needed, because a
> committee of experts was more afraid of peanuts than of being wrong.

# CASE 3: Mindguards
### Volkswagen's cheating software, 2008 to 2015

**SCREEN 1** *(image: case3-hero)*
> Volkswagen built a car smart enough to know when it was being watched.

`[ Reveal Your Case ]`

**SCREEN 2** *(image: case3-lab)*
> Volkswagen had promised the world something that didn't exist: a diesel engine that was cheap,
> powerful, and clean enough to pass California's strictest-in-the-world emissions standards.
> Their engineers tried. They couldn't build it. And the deadline was coming.

`[ What Happened Next ]`

**SCREEN 3**
> So they built something else. Hidden software that watched the steering wheel and the spinning
> tires, recognized the exact pattern of a lab emissions test, and switched the pollution controls
> **on.** Back out on a real road, it switched them **off**, releasing up to 40 times the legal
> limit of toxic nitrogen oxides.

`[ Nobody Inside Said Anything? ]`

**SCREEN 4** *(image: case3-silenced)*
> Hundreds of people inside VW knew. Their own supplier had even put a warning in writing, in 2007,
> that using this software in customer cars would be illegal. But at every level, managers acted as
> **mindguards**: engineers who raised the problem were told to fix it or stop talking, and the bad
> news never reached anyone with the power to end it. Nobody had to lie. The truth just never made
> it upstairs.

`[ So Who Finally Caught Them? ]`

**SCREEN 5** *(image: case3-roadtest)*
> 11 million cars had already been sold. It fell apart in 2014, not because of a regulator, but
> because a tiny nonprofit spent about $50,000 to drive three cars down a real highway, in a study
> meant to **prove** clean diesel worked. Final cost: over $30 billion, the CEO's job, and prison
> for engineers who were only doing what they were told.

# CASE 4: Illusion of Invulnerability
### The Mozart effect, 1993 to 2009

**SCREEN 1** *(image: case4-hero)*
> A study with no children in it changed how an entire generation of babies was raised.

`[ Reveal Your Case ]`

**SCREEN 2** *(image: case4-study)*
> In 1993, a one-page report found that college students who listened to ten minutes of Mozart did
> slightly better on a paper-folding puzzle, for about fifteen minutes afterward. That was the
> whole finding. **36 students. One puzzle. A quarter of an hour.**

`[ What Happened Next ]`

**SCREEN 3**
> The media, politicians, and parenting experts all converged on something much bigger: *Mozart
> makes babies smarter.* Nobody slowed down to check the evidence, because nobody could picture a
> downside. It's just classical music. What's the harm?

`[ How Far Did It Go? ]`

**SCREEN 4** *(image: case4-capitol)*
> That **illusion of invulnerability**, the group's certainty that it simply could not be wrong,
> turned a 36-person study into public policy. In 1998 the governor of Georgia budgeted state money
> to mail a classical recording to the parents of every baby born in the state. Florida passed a
> law requiring classical music in programs for young children.

`[ Was Any of It True? ]`

**SCREEN 5** *(image: case4-videos)*
> Baby Einstein grew into a business worth hundreds of millions, and a generation of parents judged
> themselves by how much enrichment they piped into their infants. Meanwhile, replication after
> replication found almost nothing, just a tiny bump from any music you happen to enjoy. The
> original researchers kept insisting it was never about babies or IQ. By 2009, Disney was mailing
> refunds.

## Opening lines (revealed after they find their group)

- **Case 1:** "Did you know the Cold War came down to one guy in a 120-degree submarine refusing to
  go along with his two bosses?"
- **Case 2:** "Did you know peanut allergies exploded in the U.S. *because* doctors told parents to
  avoid peanuts, with zero evidence behind it?"
- **Case 3:** "Did you know Volkswagen sold 11 million cars with software that could tell when it
  was being tested, and everyone inside who knew was told to keep quiet?"
- **Case 4:** "Did you know the whole 'classical music makes babies smarter' thing came from a study
  of 36 college students and zero babies, and it still got written into state law?"
