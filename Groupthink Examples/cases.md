# CASE 1: Direct conformity pressure
### Submarine B-59, October 1962

**SCREEN 1** *(image: case1-hero.jpg)*
> One man stood between the world and complete nuclear annihilation.

`[ Reveal Your Case ]`

**SCREEN 2** *(image: case1-submarine.jpg)*
> Inside a USSR submarine in 1962, pressure was building. While the crew was growing delirious from days within a cramped, 120°F hull, they suddenly received warning that American carriers were dropping depth charges on them. They had only minutes to react!

`[ What Happened Next ]`

**SCREEN 3**
> The captain and political officer both jumped to the same conclusion: they must fire their nuclear-armed torpedo at the Americans. Otherwise, they will be destroyed! But the sub could only fire nukes if all three commanding officers turned their keys.

`[ Who Was the Third Officer? ]`

**SCREEN 4** *(image: case1-arkhipov.jpg)*
> The captain and the officer applied **direct conformity pressure** to the third officer, Vasili Arkhipov, to fire the nuke. All three of them knew it would bring about all-out nuclear war. Fighting the pressure of his own commanding officers, Arkhipov stood firm and refused to turn his key.

`[ So What Were the Americans Doing? ]`

**SCREEN 5** *(image: case1-surfacing.jpg)*
> It turned out the Americans were dropping **practice** charges, small signaling explosives meant to make the sub surface, not sink it. Nobody was trying to kill them. Arkhipov single-handedly saved the world from nuclear war, and the U.S. government did not learn how close it came for another forty years.

`[ Now Find Your Group ]`

# CASE 2: Ignoring disconfirming evidence
### The peanut guideline, 2000

**SCREEN 1** *(image: case2-hero.jpg)*
> In 2000, the advice of doctors caused a nationwide epidemic.

`[ Reveal Your Case ]`

**SCREEN 2** *(image: case2-committee.jpg)*
> In 2000, America’s pediatricians wanted to tackle peanut allergies, which killed a small number of children each year. They had almost no research to go on, so the committee reached for the safest-sounding answer available. Keep peanuts away from babies until age 3.

`[ What Happened Next ]`

**SCREEN 3**
> Everyone fell in line. Parents, daycares, schools, and airlines pulled peanuts out of the first three years of American childhood. And over the next decade, peanut allergy in U.S. children **more than tripled.** It turns out keeping peanuts away from kids actually causes peanut allergy!

`[ Did Anyone See It Coming? ]`

**SCREEN 4** *(image: case2-bamba.jpg)*
> At the same time, doctors ignored evidence from around the world that in nations where children ate peanuts regularly, there was almost no peanut allergy. For example, rates of peanut allergy were **10 times lower** in Israel, where babies commonly ate a peanut cereal called Bamba. While **ignoring disconfirming evidence**, doctors continued to warn parents to keep peanuts away from their children.

`[ How Did It End? ]`

**SCREEN 5**
> By 2015, the science was clear: feeding babies peanuts early cut their odds of peanut allergy by **81%**. Today’s official advice is the exact reverse of the 2000 advice. Start peanuts at 4 to 6 months. An entire generation got the opposite of what it needed, because doctors were more afraid of peanuts than of being wrong.

`[ Now Find Your Group ]`

# CASE 3: Mindguards
### Volkswagen’s cheating software, 2008 to 2015

**SCREEN 1** *(image: case3-hero.jpg)*
> Volkswagen built a car smart enough to know when it was being watched.

`[ Reveal Your Case ]`

**SCREEN 2** *(image: case3-lab.jpg)*
> Volkswagen had promised the world something that did not exist: a diesel engine that was cheap, powerful, and clean enough to pass California’s strictest-in-the-world emissions standards. Their engineers tried. They could not build it. And the deadline was coming.

`[ What Happened Next ]`

**SCREEN 3**
> So they built something else. Hidden software that recognized when a car was being smog tested, and switched the pollution controls **on.** Back out on a real road, it switched them **off**, releasing up to 40 times the legal limit of toxic fumes.

`[ Nobody Inside Said Anything? ]`

**SCREEN 4** *(image: case3-silenced.jpg)*
> Hundreds of people inside VW knew. But at every level, managers acted as **mindguards**, who kept engineers from talking about the issue. The truth just never made it to the bosses in charge.

`[ So Who Finally Caught Them? ]`

**SCREEN 5** *(image: case3-roadtest.jpg)*
> The scheme fell apart in 2014, after over **11 million** highly-polluting cars had been sold. After the courts had their way with the company, Volkswagen lost over $30 billion and the CEO was fired. Some of the engineers even went to prison!

`[ Now Find Your Group ]`

# CASE 4: Illusion of invulnerability
### The Mozart effect, 1993 to 2009

**SCREEN 1** *(image: case4-hero.jpg)*
> A whole generation of parents were fooled into thinking music would turn their babies into geniuses.

`[ Reveal Your Case ]`

**SCREEN 2** *(image: case4-study.jpg)*
> In 1993, a one-page report found that college students who listened to ten minutes of Mozart did slightly better on a paper-folding puzzle, compared to students who listened to no music. On the whole, it was an unremarkable finding with only a sample size of **36 participants**.

`[ What Happened Next ]`

**SCREEN 3**
> For whatever reason, the media caught wind of the study and blew it way out of proportion. Headlines claimed Mozart can make you smarter! Soon, politicians and parenting experts piled onto the bandwagon, proclaiming that classical music can make children, and even babies, smarter.

`[ How Far Did It Go? ]`

**SCREEN 4** *(image: case4-videos.jpg)*
> These claims grew and grew. By the late 90s, millions of CDs and DVDs featuring Mozart were being sold under the Baby Einstein brand. These were meant to be played to babies and children throughout the day to improve their IQ. This was due to an **illusion of invulnerability**, with parents believing that there’s no way so many "experts" could be wrong.

`[ Was Any of It True? ]`

**SCREEN 5** *(image: case4-capitol.jpg)*
> By 1998, the governor of Georgia was mailing classical music CDs to parents of every baby born in the state. Florida passed a law requiring classical music in programs for young children. Of course, it turns out none of this had any real effect on children’s IQs. The trend finally died out in the 2000s. By 2009, the company behind Baby Einstein agreed to issue refunds to parents for their false claims.

`[ Now Find Your Group ]`
